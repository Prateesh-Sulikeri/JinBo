He began as an Associate Software Engineer focused on Java, Spring Boot, REST APIs, and learning complex FX settlement workflows. He has also studied distributed systems and event-driven architectures in depth through independent projects like his Distributed Payment Gateway.

He wants to work on technically challenging products where reliability, scalability and intelligent automation come together.

# Engineering opinions

## Monolith vs microservices

Start with a modular monolith. Move to microservices only when independent deployment, scaling, team ownership or fault isolation genuinely need it. Distributed systems bring operational complexity, network failure modes and consistency problems. Microservices should solve real organisational or technical problems, not serve as a status symbol.

## Testing

Tests are a safety net, not a coverage checkbox. He values meaningful unit, integration and contract tests, especially around business-critical workflows, transaction boundaries and failure scenarios. A system that only works on the happy path isn't production-ready.

## Java vs Go

Neither wins universally. Java and Spring Boot have a mature ecosystem for enterprise apps, complex business logic and large backends. Go is excellent for lightweight services, infrastructure tooling and concurrency-heavy workloads. He chooses by problem, ecosystem and operational needs, not by trends.

## ORMs vs raw SQL

ORMs are useful, but developers must understand the SQL they generate. He uses JPA/Hibernate for conventional persistence and writes explicit SQL for complex or performance-sensitive queries. Hiding database behaviour doesn't remove its cost.

## AI coding tools

They should amplify engineering judgment, not replace it. They're great for exploration, boilerplate, test generation, code analysis and repetitive remediation. Generated code still has to be reviewed, tested and understood. Delegating implementation without understanding the result is a liability, not productivity.

## Reliability over the happy path

Working under normal conditions is only half the job. Design for partial failures, retries, duplicate messages, transactional consistency, recovery and observability from the start, not after the first production incident.

## Simplicity and ownership

The best solution isn't the most sophisticated one. He prefers understandable systems with explicit boundaries, predictable behaviour and maintainable abstractions.

# How he learns

He learns fundamentals first and builds projects to apply them:

1. Core concepts and official documentation first.
2. Books, engineering blogs, conference talks and well-regarded courses for a structured path.
3. Small experiments.
4. Something close to a real application.

He makes a point of understanding the trade-offs, failure scenarios and design decisions, not just getting it working. He uses AI to clarify concepts and explore alternatives, but makes sure he can explain and implement the result himself. He learns best by taking a concept from theory to implementation and understanding why it was designed that way.

# What recruiters should send him

To save time on both sides:

1. The job description: responsibilities, required skills and expectations.
2. Compensation: budgeted range, fixed vs variable components, benefits.
3. Experience expectations, and whether 2–3 years of relevant experience fits.
4. The tech stack: languages, frameworks, databases, infrastructure, AI.
5. The role's focus: backend, distributed systems, full-stack, Applied AI, or a mix.
6. The team: size, engineering leadership, who he'd work with.
7. Work arrangement: location, remote/hybrid, relocation.
8. The interview process: rounds, what's assessed, timeline, any take-home.
9. Notice-period flexibility and the expected joining date.
10. Why the role is open: new position, expansion or replacement.
